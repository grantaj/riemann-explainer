AN INTERACTIVE MATHEMATICAL JOURNEY

The hidden order
of prime numbers.

How accurately can we predict the number of primes below a billion? Below a trillion? There is a surprisingly good formula. The Riemann hypothesis predicts how far that formula can be wrong — and its answer depends on where a mysterious function equals zero.

01 / THE ATOMS OF ARITHMETIC

Irregular, but not random.

A prime is an integer greater than 1 whose only positive divisors are 1 and itself. Every integer greater than 1 can be built by multiplying primes, and its prime factorisation is unique apart from the order: for example, 60 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5.

A window into the integers

Teal marks are primes; faint marks are composite integers (or 1). Each row contains 30 integers. The chart below shows the gap after each prime (plotted at that prime’s position). A gap of 2 means that the next prime is two integers away.

Move the window towards 10,000. Primes generally become less frequent, but gaps remain irregular. The prime number theorem tells us that their average density on a large scale is roughly 1/log x. It does not predict the location of the next prime.

Explore further Why primes never run out

Suppose we had a complete finite list of primes. Multiply them together and add 1. The result leaves remainder 1 when divided by any prime on the list, yet every integer greater than 1 has a prime divisor. There must be another prime. This proves primes never stop, but says little about how often they occur.

Mathematical details

Writing pₙ for the nth prime, the prime number theorem π(x) ∼ x/log x is equivalent to pₙ ∼ n log n. Thus the average of the first n−1 gaps, (pₙ−2)/(n−1), is asymptotic to log n and also log pₙ. This says nothing about the size of an individual gap or an arbitrary short interval.

02 / A STATISTICAL LAW

Counting the primes.

Rather than predict the next prime, let's count how many primes we have passed. Write π(x) for the number of primes up to x. For example, π(10) = 4 because 2, 3, 5 and 7 are prime. Here π is the name of a function, not the number 3.14159.

The prime number theorem says that for large x a good estimate is x/log x (log means the natural logarithm). A more refined approximation, the logarithmic integral, or li(x), adds up the gradually decreasing estimated density of primes rather than treating it as constant.

Staircase and smooth curves

π(x)x/log xli(x)

Try x = 10,000. There are exactly 1,229 primes. The simpler approximation gives about 1,086; the logarithmic integral gives about 1,246. Turn on the error view to see the difference. The central question is not simply whether the approximation is good, but how fast its error can grow.

Explore further Where does the logarithmic integral come from?

Imagine that near a large number t, roughly one integer in log t is prime. The estimated density is 1/log t. Adding small rectangles under this density curve estimates the count over an interval. An integral is the limit of that process.

The usual li(x) adds a fixed constant to this area, which becomes relatively unimportant at large x.

Mathematical details

The asymptotic equivalence f(x) ∼ g(x) means f(x)/g(x) → 1. The prime number theorem is

π(x) ∼ x/log x ∼ li(x).

By l'Hôpital's rule li(x) ∼ x/log x. Repeated integration by parts yields li(x)=x/log x+x/log²x+O(x/log³x), explaining the refined approximation. These asymptotics are not a pointwise probability law for primes.

li(x) = PV ∫0x dt/log t = li(2) + ∫2x dt/log t   (x>1).

PV is the Cauchy principal value at the singularity 1. NIST DLMF: logarithmic integral ↗

03 / MULTIPLICATION BECOMES ANALYSIS

One function. Every prime.

We need a way to study all primes at once. Euler discovered one by looking at a surprising sum. Square each positive integer, take its reciprocal, and add:

1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + 1/16 + 1/25 + ⋯ = 1.644934…

Replace the exponent 2 with any real number s greater than 1. The result is the zeta function, written ζ(s) (the Greek letter zeta).

ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + 1/4s + ⋯

Here is the surprise. We can obtain the same value using only primes, by multiplying a factor for each prime. This is the Euler product.

ζ(s) = 1/(1−2−s) × 1/(1−3−s) × 1/(1−5−s) × ⋯

Each factor expands into powers of one prime. Multiplying the factors combines those powers into every possible positive integer, exactly once, by unique factorisation. For example, the terms for 2 and 3 multiply to produce the term for 6.

Explore further Discover the Euler product

Set s=2 and use only primes 2 and 3. Their factors are the geometric series 1+1/4+1/16+⋯ and 1+1/9+1/81+⋯. Select a term from each to form a number 2ᵃ3ᵇ.

5 is missing. Include its factor and it appears, together with 10, 15 and 30. In the limit every positive integer appears once.

Mathematical details

For complex s with σ=Re(s)>1, the series converges absolutely since |n−s|=n−σ. For a finite set of primes P, absolute convergence permits expansion:

∏p∈P(1−p−s)−1 = ∑n: p|n ⇒ p∈P n−s.

Let P exhaust the primes and use dominated convergence. Also ∑p,k≥1p−ks/k converges absolutely and locally uniformly. Its exponential equals ζ(s), proving nonvanishing in σ>1 and justifying logarithmic differentiation later. NIST DLMF: Euler product ↗

Two routes to the same limit

Partial sumPartial productζ(s)

Try reducing s towards 1. Both approximations converge more slowly; at s=1 the sum 1+1/2+1/3+⋯ has no finite limit. We have encoded every prime in a single function. Riemann's insight was to study this function in a much larger landscape than the ordinary number line.

04 / INTO THE COMPLEX PLANE

When numbers become waves.

So far the input s has been a point on a number line. Imagine letting it move around a plane. A complex number s = σ + it has two coordinates: σ (sigma) is horizontal and t is vertical. The symbol i satisfies i² = −1.

Now the terms of the zeta sum become arrows, not just positive numbers. The horizontal coordinate σ controls the lengths of the output arrows. The vertical coordinate t controls their angles, which are different for each term. As t changes, the arrows rotate at different rates and can reinforce or cancel — much like waves.

Explore further Why a complex exponent makes a wave

Euler's formula says exp(iθ)=cos θ+i sin θ, an arrow of length 1 at angle θ. Therefore

n−s = n−σ[cos(t log n) − i sin(t log n)].

The second term has length 2−σ and angle −t log 2; the third has length 3−σ and angle −t log 3. They turn at different rates. The logarithmic scale will reappear when we reconstruct the primes.

Mathematical details

For σ>1, ζ(σ+it) is exactly the Fourier transform (negative-exponential convention) of the finite measure ∑n≥1n−σδlog n. The variable t probes oscillations on a logarithmic scale. This identity does not alone justify continuing the divergent series into σ≤1, or establish the explicit formula.

At t=0 every arrow points right. As t changes, they can partly cancel. If the full sum were exactly zero, both output coordinates would vanish. Such an input is a zero of the function. The Euler product guarantees there are no zeros where σ>1, but the function can be extended beyond this region.

How can a function extend beyond where its sum works? Consider the simpler example

1 + u + u² + u³ + ⋯ = 1/(1−u)   (|u|<1)

The sum on the left works only when |u|<1. The formula on the right works even at u=2, where its value is −1, although the original sum diverges.

The same function, beyond its series

For |u|<1 the finite sum approaches the rational function. Move beyond 1 or −1; the rational expression still makes sense away from its pole.

Real-axis slice of analytic continuation. The rational formula, not the divergent series, defines the extension; large values are clipped and the pole is not connected across.

This is the idea of analytic continuation: extend the function, not the divergent sum.

Explore further How can zeta be continued?

Instead of adding all the terms, alternate their signs. For real s>0 the alternating sum converges. At s=1 it gives 1−1/2+1/3−1/4+⋯=log 2.

η(s)=1−2−s+3−s−4−s+⋯

Separating the even terms of the original sum gives η(s)=(1−21−s)ζ(s). Where the denominator is nonzero, the quotient provides a formula for ζ in a larger region.

Mathematical details

For Re(s)>1, summation by parts gives ζ(s)=s∫1∞⌊x⌋x−s−1dx. Write ⌊x⌋=x−{x}:

ζ(s)=s/(s−1)−s∫1∞{x}x−s−1dx.

The integral converges locally uniformly for Re(s)>0, giving a meromorphic continuation with residue 1 at s=1. Euler–Maclaurin summation extends it to the whole plane without further poles. The identity theorem ensures uniqueness; apparent nonreal singularities of the eta quotient in this region are removable.

ξ(s)=½s(s−1)π−s/2Γ(s/2)ζ(s)

The completed function ξ is entire, satisfies ξ(s)=ξ(1−s) and ξ(s̄) = ξ(s)̄, and its zeros are precisely the nontrivial zeros of ζ. Gamma factors explain the negative even zeros. Nonvanishing on Re(s)=1 is a substantive theorem, not a consequence of the symmetry alone. NIST DLMF: functional equation ↗ · Zeros ↗

The extended zeta function is defined everywhere except at s=1, where it becomes unbounded (a simple pole). Its zeros at −2, −4, −6, … are called trivial zeros. Every other zero lies between the vertical lines σ=0 and σ=1, the critical strip.

A symmetry called the functional equation pairs nontrivial zeros across the middle line σ=½. There is also symmetry above and below the real axis. The map initially shows the first six nontrivial zeros in the upper half-plane.

Notice that every marked nontrivial zero lies on the line σ = ½. Is this merely a pattern in the first few zeros, or is it true for every zero? Before stating the hypothesis, we need to understand what these zeros have to do with the primes.

05 / THE MUSIC OF THE PRIMES

Zeros make the staircase.

Here is the heart of the story. A complex sound can be built by adding simple waves. Something similar happens with the irregular staircase of primes: it can be reconstructed from waves supplied by the zeros of zeta.

How waves build a shape

The grey target is a square wave. Add sine waves to watch smooth curves begin to reproduce sharp jumps.

This is an analogy, not the prime formula: prime waves have different frequencies and amplitudes and oscillate in log x.

For the actual formula it is convenient to count prime powers as well as primes, giving each power of p a weight of log p. This staircase is called the Chebyshev function, ψ(x) (psi). It still records the position of every prime, and its smooth approximation is especially simple: the straight line x.

Riemann's explicit formula tells us how to correct that straight line. Each nontrivial zero supplies a wave. Adding all the waves, with two small corrections, reconstructs the staircase.

One zero, one wave

Choose a zero to see the wave it contributes together with its mirror below the real axis. Its imaginary coordinate sets the frequency in log x; its real coordinate sets the growth of the amplitude.

Now add these waves together. With no zero terms included we have the smooth trend plus the two small corrections. Adding more brings out finer details of the actual staircase. Knowing the zeros gives us information about where the primes are.

What is actually being counted?

Each prime power gets the logarithm of its underlying prime, so 8 and 2 contribute the same amount. Ordinary counting includes only primes.

The step heights are exact log p. Prime powers can be removed to recover ordinary prime locations.

Explore further The exact formula and what each term means

The weighted staircase is

ψ(x)=∑pᵏ≤x, k≥1 log p.

Up to 10, the prime powers are 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8 and 9, so ψ(10)=3 log 2+2 log 3+log 5+log 7. Its jumps locate prime powers; retaining only first powers recovers the primes.

For x>1, the Riemann–von Mangoldt formula is

ψ0(x)=x−∑ρxρ/ρ−log(2π)−½log(1−x−2).

ψ₀ takes the midpoint at jumps. The sum is over all nontrivial zeros with multiplicity, in symmetric height order. A conjugate pair ρ=β±iγ contributes the real wave

−2 Re(xβ+iγ/(β+iγ)).

Equivalently, −2xβcos(γ log x−arg(β+iγ))/√(β²+γ²). The amplitude grows like xβ; the angular frequency in log x is γ.

Mathematical details

Logarithmically differentiate the absolutely and locally uniformly convergent Euler product in Re(s)>1:

−ζ′(s)/ζ(s)=∑p,k≥1(log p)p−ks=∑n≥1Λ(n)n−s.

Here Λ(pᵏ)=log p for prime powers and Λ(n)=0 otherwise. Perron's inversion formula with c>1 gives the weighted count (half weight at a jump):

ψ0(x)=limT→∞ (1/(2πi))∫c−iTc+iT[−ζ′(s)/ζ(s)]xs/s ds.

The inversion kernel gives the step function of x/n, with value ½ at x=n. Shifting the contour left collects residues: s=1 gives x; a nontrivial zero ρ of multiplicity m gives −mxρ/ρ; s=0 gives −ζ′(0)/ζ(0)=−log(2π); and trivial zeros −2k give x−2k/(2k), summing to −½log(1−x−2).

This is a derivation outline, not a substitute for analytic estimates: the contour shift requires heights avoiding zeros and bounds on ζ′/ζ and the displaced integrals. These justify the symmetric limiting sum. The formula does not assume RH. NIST DLMF: explicit formula ↗ · John Baez: exposition ↗

06 / THE CENTRAL QUESTION

Why exactly one half?

We can now state the Riemann hypothesis: every nontrivial zero ρ of ζ lies exactly on the critical line.

Re(ρ) = ½.

Why one half? A zero whose horizontal coordinate is ½ contributes a wave growing like √x. A hypothetical zero at ¾ would contribute a wave growing like x¾. The difference in the exponent seems small, but becomes enormous for large x.

Hypothetical zeros, not observations. The chart isolates the right-hand conjugate pair at fixed γ; the reflected pair is shown in the plane but not included in this envelope. At β = ½ the reflected points coincide.

We have come full circle. The Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the prediction that the error in counting primes cannot grow much faster than the square root of the size of the numbers being counted. Precisely, it is bounded by a constant times √x log x for all sufficiently large x. This is a remarkably strong constraint on the apparent irregularity of primes.

Checking finitely many zeros cannot establish the bound for arbitrarily large x. A proof must rule out every possible off-line zero.

Explore further The precise error bound

RH is equivalent to each estimate (as x tends to infinity):

π(x)=li(x)+O(√x log x)
ψ(x)=x+O(√x log²x).

The big-O notation hides a constant independent of x. The bounds describe the combined effect of all zeros, not one pair.

Mathematical details

RH ⇒ error bound. A standard truncated explicit formula with N(T)=O(T log T) gives ψ(x)−x=O(√x log²x) under RH. The sum of 1/|ρ| up to height T is O(log²T); choosing a suitable height and controlling the remainder is essential. Simply adding infinitely many individual amplitude bounds would not work. Higher prime powers contribute O(√x log x) or less; partial summation gives the π estimate.

Error bound ⇒ RH. For Re(s)>1,

−ζ′(s)/ζ(s)=s∫1∞ψ(x)x−s−1dx.

Subtract s/(s−1), the contribution of x. If ψ(x)−x=O(√x log²x), the remaining integral is analytic for Re(s)>½. Thus −ζ′/ζ cannot have poles there except at 1. There are no zeros to the right of ½, and symmetry excludes those to the left. The π estimate implies the ψ estimate by partial summation and bounding higher prime powers.
