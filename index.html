AN INTERACTIVE MATHEMATICAL JOURNEY

The hidden order
of prime numbers.

Prime numbers appear irregularly, yet their distribution follows a remarkably precise law. The Riemann hypothesis is a statement about how far their actual distribution can stray from that law. We will see why this question leads to a function of a complex variable.

01 / THE ATOMS OF ARITHMETIC

Irregular, but not random.

A prime is an integer greater than 1 whose only positive divisors are 1 and itself. Every integer greater than 1 can be built by multiplying primes, and its prime factorisation is unique apart from the order: for example, 60 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5.

A window into the integers

Teal marks are primes; faint marks are composite integers (or 1). Each row contains 30 integers. The chart below shows the gap after each prime (plotted at that prime’s position). A gap of 2 means that the next prime is two integers away.

Move the window towards 10,000. Primes generally become less frequent, but gaps remain irregular. The prime number theorem tells us that their average density on a large scale is roughly 1/log x. It does not predict the location of the next prime.

02 / A STATISTICAL LAW

Counting the primes.

Instead of trying to predict individual primes, count them. Write π(x) for the number of primes not exceeding x: π(10) = 4, since 2, 3, 5 and 7 are prime. The resulting graph is a staircase. The prime number theorem says that, on large scales, it follows x/log x.

π(x) ∼ x / log x ∼ li(x)

The symbol ∼ means that the ratio tends to 1 as x grows. Here log means the natural logarithm. The logarithmic integral, li(x), improves on x/log x by adding up the predicted density 1/log t as t increases. Both approximations have the same leading behaviour, but li(x) accounts for more of the variation.

Staircase and smooth curves

π(x)x/log xli(x)

At x = 100, there are 25 primes. The two approximations give about 21.7 and 30.1. Turn on the error view: instead of counting primes, we now measure how far the smooth curves miss the staircase. How large can that discrepancy become as x grows? This is the question we will connect to Riemann's hypothesis.

03 / MULTIPLICATION BECOMES ANALYSIS

One function. Every prime.

To study primes, Euler introduced a remarkable bridge between multiplication and addition. Start with a real number s > 1 and add 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + ⋯. The sum is finite and defines the zeta function, ζ(s). Euler showed that the same value can be obtained using only primes:

ζ(s) = ∑n≥1 n−s = ∏p prime (1 − p−s)−1

The factor for p = 2 is 1 + 2−s + 4−s + 8−s + ⋯; the factor for 3 is similar. Multiplying these geometric series picks out every integer exactly once by unique factorisation. This is the Euler product: a function defined using all integers contains information about every prime.

Two routes to the same limit

Partial sumPartial productζ(s)

At s = 2 the common limit is π²/6. Try reducing s towards 1: the terms shrink more slowly, and more are needed to approach the limit. At s = 1 the harmonic series diverges. To extract more detailed information about primes, Riemann's crucial move was to let s be complex, not just real.

04 / INTO THE COMPLEX PLANE

When numbers become waves.

With complex numbers, a number has two coordinates. Write s = σ + it, where i² = −1. The real part σ is its horizontal coordinate; the imaginary part t is its vertical coordinate. Now the terms of the zeta series are complex too:

n−s = n−σ · exp(−it log n)

Each term is a vector in the complex plane. Its length is n−σ, and its angle is −t log n radians (by Euler's formula). So changing t rotates the vectors at different rates. Adding them head to tail gives a partial sum of ζ(s).

When t = 0, every term points right. For t ≠ 0, the terms point in different directions and can partly cancel. But the Euler product ensures ζ(s) cannot be zero when σ > 1. To find its zeros (inputs where its output is exactly zero), we need to go beyond the region where the original series converges.

analytic continuation gives ζ(s) a unique meaning throughout the complex plane, except at s = 1, where it has a simple pole. The continued function agrees with the original series wherever that series converges. This is not a way of summing a divergent series in the ordinary sense.

There are zeros at −2, −4, −6, …, called trivial zeros. All the others, called nontrivial zeros, lie inside the critical strip, between σ = 0 and σ = 1. A symmetry called the functional equation pairs nontrivial zeros across σ = ½; complex conjugation pairs them across the real axis.

Notice that every marked nontrivial zero lies on the line σ = ½. Is this merely a pattern in the first few zeros, or is it true for every zero? Before stating the hypothesis, we need to understand what these zeros have to do with the primes.

05 / THE MUSIC OF THE PRIMES

Zeros make the staircase.

The Euler product connects ζ to primes, but how do the zeros enter the picture? Differentiating the logarithm of that product gives −ζ′(s)/ζ(s), a sum over prime powers. This suggests counting prime powers instead of just primes. The Chebyshev function, ψ(x), counts every power p, p², p³, … with weight log p.

ψ(x) = ∑pᵏ≤x log p

For example, ψ(10) = 3 log 2 + 2 log 3 + log 5 + log 7, because 2, 4, 8, 3, 9, 5 and 7 are prime powers up to 10. Like π(x), ψ is a staircase, but its smooth approximation is especially simple: x. The explicit formula recovers the staircase from the zeros of ζ:

ψ0(x) = x − ∑ρ xρ/ρ − log(2π) − ½ log(1−x−2).

Here ρ runs over all nontrivial zeros, counted with multiplicity and summed symmetrically in height. ψ₀ agrees with ψ except at a jump, where it takes the midpoint. The x term is the smooth trend; the two logarithmic corrections account for the remaining contributions. The sum over the zeros supplies the fluctuations. Each conjugate pair ρ = β ± iγ gives a real wave:

−2 Re(xβ+iγ/(β+iγ)).

The imaginary part γ determines the frequency of the wave in log x. The real part β determines how fast its amplitude grows: proportional to xβ. Try adding zeros one at a time. Their waves combine to approximate the staircase. This is the mathematical connection we have been looking for.

06 / THE CENTRAL QUESTION

Why exactly one half?

We can now state the Riemann hypothesis: every nontrivial zero ρ of ζ lies exactly on the critical line.

Re(ρ) = ½.

Why does that matter? A zero at β = ½ contributes a wave whose amplitude grows like √x. A zero at β = ¾ contributes a wave growing like x¾, which eventually becomes much larger. The functional equation would require a partner at 1−β, but that symmetry alone does not rule out such a quartet. Move a hypothetical pair to see the effect.

Riemann's hypothesis is equivalent to a precise bound on the error in counting primes:

π(x) = li(x) + O(√x log x)

In big-O notation, this means that beyond some point the absolute error is at most a constant times √x log x. The corresponding bound for ψ is ψ(x) = x + O(√x log²x). These are theorems about the combined effect of all zeros, not just one pair. The hypothesis does not say where each prime occurs; it sharply constrains how irregular their collective distribution can be.

This takes us back to the discrepancy in Chapter 2. Checking finitely many zeros, no matter how many, cannot establish this bound for arbitrarily large x. A proof must rule out every possible off-line zero.
