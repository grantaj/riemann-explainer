01 / THE ATOMS OF ARITHMETIC

Irregular, but not random.

A prime is an integer greater than 1 whose only positive divisors are 1 and itself. Every integer greater than 1 has a unique factorisation into primes, up to order. This is why they are the natural building blocks of multiplication.

A window into the integers

Teal marks are primes; faint marks are composite integers (or 1). Each row contains 30 integers. The gap chart shows distances between successive primes in this window.

There is no simple repeating sequence of primes. Yet the average gap near a large number x is roughly log x, in the asymptotic sense supplied by the prime number theorem. Local gaps can differ dramatically.

02 / THE LAW OF LARGE NUMBERS

Counting the primes.

Write π(x) for the number of primes not exceeding x. It is a staircase. The prime number theorem says that π(x) is asymptotic to x/log x. Another smooth approximation, li(x), is typically much closer at large x.

π(x) ∼ x / log x ∼ li(x)

Here li(x) is the logarithmic integral, interpreted as a principal value at 1. The symbol ∼ says that the ratio tends to 1, not that the difference tends to zero.

Staircase and smooth curves

The next question is the important one: how large can the error become? The prime number theorem by itself does not give the sharp answer.

03 / MULTIPLICATION BECOMES ANALYSIS

One function. Every prime.

For real s > 1 (and, more generally, complex s with real part greater than 1), the zeta function is defined by an absolutely convergent series. Euler discovered that it also equals an infinite product indexed by primes.

ζ(s) = ∑n≥1 n−s = ∏p prime (1 − p−s)−1

Expand each factor as a geometric series. Multiplying them selects every possible combination of prime powers, and unique factorisation says each positive integer appears exactly once. Thus a function built from all integers encodes all the primes.

Two routes to the same limit

Partial sumPartial productζ(s)

At s = 2 the common limit is π²/6. As s approaches 1 from above, convergence slows and the function grows without bound. The product is nonzero throughout Re(s) > 1; the interesting zeros lie elsewhere.

04 / INTO THE COMPLEX PLANE

From a sum to a landscape.

Let s = σ + it. The individual summands become rotating vectors: n−s = n−σ exp(−it log n). The real part controls length; the imaginary part controls phase.

A single summand as a vector

The series no longer converges when σ ≤ 1. But analytic continuation extends ζ uniquely to a meromorphic function on the whole complex plane, with just a simple pole at s = 1. This is not a claim that the original series converges there. The completed function

ξ(s) = ½s(s−1)π−s/2 Γ(s/2)ζ(s)

is entire and satisfies ξ(s) = ξ(1−s). Complex conjugation gives another symmetry. The negative even integers −2, −4, … are the trivial zeros of ζ. All other zeros lie in the critical strip 0 < σ < 1; reflection and conjugation pair them across the critical line and the real axis.

05 / THE MUSIC OF THE PRIMES

Zeros make the staircase.

The link to primes becomes especially clean if we count prime powers with a weight. The Chebyshev function ψ jumps by log p at each p, p², p³, … . It is related to π through partial summation and removal of the higher prime powers.

ψ(x) = ∑pᵏ≤x log p

For x > 1 the Riemann–von Mangoldt explicit formula is

ψ0(x) = x − ∑ρ xρ/ρ − log(2π) − ½ log(1−x−2).

The sum runs over nontrivial zeros with multiplicity, in symmetric height order. ψ₀ equals ψ except at jumps, where it takes the midpoint. The term x is the smooth trend. Every conjugate pair ρ = β ± iγ supplies an oscillation:

−2 Re(xβ+iγ/(β+iγ)).

Its phase oscillates with log x, and its amplitude grows like xβ. This is why the horizontal position of a zero matters as much as its height.

06 / THE CENTRAL QUESTION

Why exactly one half?

The Riemann hypothesis states that every nontrivial zero ρ of ζ satisfies

Re(ρ) = ½.

Reflection symmetry alone does not force zeros onto the line. An off-line zero with β > ½ would have a partner at 1−β and both would have conjugates. Its contribution to the explicit formula could grow faster than the square-root scale.

β = ½: envelopeSelected β: envelope

Hypothetical zeros, not observations. The chart isolates the right-hand conjugate pair at fixed γ; the reflected pair is shown in the plane but not included in this envelope. At β = ½ the reflected points coincide.

RH is equivalent to a strong error estimate for prime counting: π(x) = li(x) + O(√x log x). An equivalent bound for ψ(x) is ψ(x) = x + O(√x log²x). These are asymptotic theorems about the collective contribution of the zeros, not estimates obtained by fitting a finite graph.

Checking even billions of zeros cannot establish a statement about all of them. A proof has to rule out every possible off-line zero.
