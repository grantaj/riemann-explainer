PART II / THE BRIDGE TO THE PROOF

How do you prove the primes behave?

Part I showed that a zero off the line would make the primes fluctuate too much. Now we must bound that fluctuation. The remarkable idea is to use divisibility versus size: construct an arithmetic object with many known prime factors, and independently measure how large it can be.

We start with integers, discover exactly why they are not good enough, and then repair the defects one at a time.

Verification, 24 November 2026. On 21 November the Lean FRO and Mathlib Initiative certified the Alpöge–Claude proof of RH after independent checks. Independent checks also cover its Dirichlet GRH statements. A conventional readable proof is still being developed. Our explanations are a reading aid, not an independent replay.

07 / CHANGE THE QUESTION

Make the staircase smooth.

In Part I, we counted each prime power as a step. Now let each prime power start a ramp instead. This gives more weight to small prime powers and makes the sum continuous, which makes it easier to analyse.

From steps to ramps

The weighted ramp for n starts at x=n, with slope Λ(n)/n. Removing the smooth main term reveals the error, not the size of the count.

The resulting error is the quantity the proof wants to control. Its natural scale is roughly the square root of x.

Explore further The exact quantity

The von Mangoldt function Λ(n) equals log p if n is a positive power of a prime p, and zero otherwise. Let γ be Euler's constant, not a zero ordinate.

G(x) = Σn≤x Λ(n)(x/n−1) − x log x + (1+γ)x

For example, at x=6 the ramp sum has contributions from 2, 3, 4 and 5. The term for 4 is log 2 × (6/4−1).

Mathematical details

For Re(s)>1 the integral of a single ramp against x−s−1 equals n−s/[s(s−1)], by integrating from n to infinity. Consequently the Mellin transform of G, integrated from 1 to infinity, is

−ζ′(s)/[ζ(s)s(s−1)] − 1/(s−1)² + (1+γ)/(s−1).

The centering cancels the singularity at 1. At a nontrivial zero ρ of multiplicity m the residue is −m/[ρ(ρ−1)]. Smoothing has not erased the zeros. Author's announcement, pp. 3–4; source atlas, components 4–5.

Our task: the error G(x) should be at most roughly √x. We already know that controlling it on sufficiently long windows implies RH; the next problem is to find an arithmetic reason why it cannot be large. Start with the simplest possible construction ↓

08 / THE FIRST ATTEMPT

An integer cannot hide its prime factors.

If a nonzero integer is divisible by 3, 5 and 7, its absolute value must be at least 105. Knowing which primes divide a number gives a lower bound on its size. Conversely, knowing its size limits the primes that can divide it. This elementary observation drives the new proof.

∑p ep log p ≤ log |N|

Chebyshev applied this idea to the central binomial coefficient. Every prime between n and 2n divides it, and it is no greater than 4ⁿ. But this gives a bound too coarse for RH.

Divisibility versus size

This elementary bound is the starting point of the much stronger arithmetic comparison, not the comparison itself. See Rubinstein §I.2.

Why the factorial falls short

The factorial n! contains every prime power below n. Replace the integer number of multiples of each prime power by the smooth approximation n/r. Their accumulated difference is the problem.

Exact finite sums using Legendre’s formula. The floor error is comparable to n, not a small fixed error.

Explore further Where the missing error comes from
log(n!) = ∑r≤n Λ(r)⌊n/r⌋

Replacing the floor by n/r costs R(n)=∑Λ(r){n/r}. This is O(n), and the prime number theorem gives R(n)∼(1−γ)n. A term of order n is much larger than the required square-root error. The smooth ramp sum is n∑Λ(r)/r−ψ(n); the floor identity alone cannot separate ψ(n) accurately enough.

Mathematical details

Derived The floor identity follows by counting multiples of p, p², … in the factorial.

Using ∑r≤nΛ(r)/r=log n−γ+o(1) and Stirling's formula gives the stated asymptotic for R(n). See §I.2 ↗.

We need a more flexible arithmetic object whose divisibility is closely related to the smooth sum. One source of such objects is an elliptic curve. First, see why primes behave differently in two dimensions.

09 / A DIFFERENT ARITHMETIC

Some primes split. Others don't.

Allow numbers of the form a+bi with a and b integers. They are the Gaussian integers, drawn on a square grid. Some ordinary primes now factor: 5=(2+i)(2−i). Others, such as 3, stay prime. We call the latter inert. The new proof uses related number systems in which chosen primes remain inert and force strong divisibility.

Factor a prime in the Gaussian integers

Explore further Why the residue class matters

For an odd prime p, a factorization in Gaussian integers is equivalent to writing p as a sum of two integer squares. This is possible exactly when p leaves remainder 1 on division by 4. Primes leaving remainder 3 stay prime. The prime 2 is exceptional: it ramifies.

Mathematical details

ℤ[i]/(p) ≅ 𝔽ₚ[X]/(X²+1). For p≡3 mod 4 the quadratic is irreducible, giving the field 𝔽ₚ². For p≡1 mod 4 it has distinct roots, giving a product of two fields. In the proof, complex multiplication (extra endomorphisms of elliptic curves) and the action of Frobenius at inert primes enter divisibility estimates in algebraic coordinates. This Gaussian example is only a model of splitting. See Rubinstein §§I.3–I.4 and III.5.

The same splitting pattern governs the arithmetic of an elliptic function associated with the figure-eight curve. That gives us a concrete integer to study.

10 / THE WORKED EXAMPLE

A curve that produces divisible integers.

Ordinary sine turns an arc length on a circle into a coordinate. The lemniscate sine does something related for a figure-eight curve. Its derivatives at zero produce surprising integers. Unlike the earlier examples, here the same number supplies both divisibility and analytic size.

From arc length to a number

Move along one lobe of the curve. The radial coordinate t is the lemniscate sine of the distance s travelled from the origin.

The arc length s=∫₀ᵗ du/√(1−u⁴); sl(s)=t. The curve picture is the geometric interpretation, not the elliptic curve's affine (x,y) equation.

Discover the integers

The nth derivative Aₙ of sl at zero is an integer. Change n; compare its actual prime exponents with the predicted lower bound.

The integer is computed with exact BigInt arithmetic. The bound for inert primes and the asymptotic size are theorems in the cited reconstruction; this is a worked example, not part of the actual RH proof.

Write f=sl, with f(0)=0, f′(0)=1 and f″=−2f³. Differentiate n times and use the product rule. Starting with A₀=0 and A₁=1 gives each next derivative as an integer combination of earlier ones.

An+2 = −2∑i+j+k=n (n!/(i!j!k!)) AiAjAk

For example A₅=−12, A₉=3024 and A₁₃=−4390848. Rotational symmetry forces the other orders to vanish. This also follows inductively from the recurrence.

11 / REPAIR THE SIZE

Replace the integer by maps.

The lemniscate experiment shows why an integer's total size is too large for the part we can control. The proof changes the object to a lattice map, whose index measures divisibility and whose norm can be estimated independently. The replacement is built using theta functions: weighted sums over lattices. A lattice is a regular grid of points, and a Gaussian weight makes distant points contribute very little.

A Gaussian lattice sum

Taking derivatives inserts powers of each lattice point into the sum. Symmetry causes many contributions to cancel. The proof exploits much richer theta kernels in which both arithmetic divisibility and analytic size can be estimated. A theta lift is a transformation built by pairing a function with such a kernel; composing these maps creates the multiplicity-like gain.

Explore further A precise toy theta function
Θ(z)=∑λ∈ℤ[i] exp(−π|λ|²+λz)

Its nth derivative at zero is the sum of λⁿ exp(−π|λ|²). Rotating the grid by 90 degrees multiplies the sum by iⁿ, so it vanishes unless n is a multiple of four. The plot uses a symmetric finite square; teal and orange show positive and negative real contributions, with purple for purely imaginary contributions. Magnitudes are scaled within each plot.

Mathematical details

The Gaussian ensures locally uniform absolute convergence and permits termwise differentiation. In the polynomial Gaussian inner product, ∥zᵃ∥²=∫₀∞ tᵃe−tdt=a!, explaining why factorial size enters the analytic estimate. For an integral full-rank square matrix A, the index [ℤᵈ:Aℤᵈ]=|det A|; Hadamard’s inequality bounds this by the product of column norms. This illustrates how index and size can meet, not the main inequality itself.

In the actual construction, the spaces consist of automorphic forms on definite unitary groups with Hermitian lattices. The required inputs include integrality of theta kernels, local valuation gains, inner-product and covolume formulas, rank and trace estimates, denominator bounds and the assembly of (M). These are not proved by the square-grid model. Rubinstein §§I.4–I.7, III.4–III.9.

The bridge to the weighted prime sum

A schematic local gain has the form max(a/pʰ−w,0) at an inert prime, for odd h, before loss terms. Write a=xw. Dividing by w gives exactly max(x/pʰ−1,0), the ramp in Chapter 7.

Schematic main term only. Actual weights vary by place and the formal inequality includes losses. Source: Rubinstein §I.4.3, (I.19)–(I.21).

12 / REPAIR ROUNDING

Why a chain helps.

We have replaced our integer by a lattice map. Now suppose a fixed rounding or setup cost is shared across m maps, while the comparison introduces a growing cost proportional to m. The normalized cost has terms x/m and m. Alpöge's announcement describes adapting a method that counts solutions of polynomial equations over finite fields. The trick is to impose many conditions at once, then use algebraic structure to control their cost.

Cost exponent aCost exponent 1−aLarger exponent

This simplified optimization explains the square-root scale, not why the real construction satisfies these cost estimates.

Explore further Multiplicity, theta lifts and arithmetic geometry

A polynomial with a root of multiplicity m has m factors of (t−r). If a nonzero polynomial of degree d vanishes to order m at N distinct points, then mN≤d. Stepanov's method uses an auxiliary polynomial and vanishing conditions to bound finite-field point counts. Integers do not supply a comparable derivative directly.

Spend the degree budget

The proposed analogy uses a chain of theta lifts, transformations between spaces of automorphic forms, in place of repeated vanishing. Automorphic forms are functions with prescribed transformation symmetries; arithmetic information is encoded in those symmetries. Galois representations encode symmetries of algebraic numbers by matrices. Galois cohomology measures obstructions to solving compatible arithmetic conditions. These are separate tools in the construction, not different names for multiplicity.

Mathematical details

The reading atlas identifies the theta chain and its arithmetic estimates (topDesWin, RankVal), the finite-character comparison (core_gm, ch_top), host supply (supplyChi) and a cohomological window (VWinAt). A representative loss exponent is ½+9ε, where ε can be chosen after θ. These are identifiers for following the formal argument, not derivations here.

In the simplified balance, max(a,1−a)≥½ with equality only at a=½. This does not establish any of the comparison, divisibility, covolume or cohomological estimates. Announcement; components 8–14; Rubinstein's ongoing reconstruction.

The actual proof constructs a compatible family of maps; its full error has four terms, not just these two. There is one remaining obstacle: these maps naturally detect selected primes. Recover the other primes ↓

13 / REPAIR MISSING PRIMES

Recover all the primes.

The lemniscate detects primes that stay prime in the Gaussian integers, not every prime. By choosing other quadratic fields and using character weights, the actual proof can compare restricted counts with the original count. For example, every prime greater than 3 is either 1 or 2 modulo 3. A Dirichlet character is a multiplicative way of weighting these classes. Its associated L-function is a cousin of the zeta function.

Characters modulo 5

Nonzero residues are powers of 2 modulo 5: 1, 2, 4, 3. Multiples of 5 always receive weight zero. Averaging the four characters keeps only residue 1.

Explore further Why the proof needs families

Characters act like frequency filters on residue classes. Their orthogonality allows an arithmetic count to be rewritten as a sum of character-weighted prime counts. The proof uses specially constructed families, not just this toy group of four.

Mathematical details
L(s,χ)=Σn≥1χ(n)n−s = ∏p(1−χ(p)p−s)−1

For Re(s)>1, the logarithmic derivative is −L′/L=Σ Λ(n)χ(n)n−s. At shared zeros the residue contributions of positively weighted L-functions cannot cancel: their multiplicities are positive integers. The real-part prime sums use conjugates as well. The actual construction must ensure suitable families containing the target character, with the appropriate centering and uniform window estimate. Components 4–5, 8 and 10.

The construction now has the right kind of error bound. We still need the analytic implication: why would controlling G on whole windows rule out an off-line zero?

14 / CLOSE THE ANALYTIC LOOP

Less than a global bound.

Here is a surprising feature of the proof: it is enough to bound the error from above, and only across certain long intervals that keep appearing arbitrarily far out. The bound does not need to hold between those intervals.

Schematic exponents, plotted on logarithmic axes; shaded intervals are example windows. An envelope alone does not prove a contradiction, because zero terms oscillate and interact.

Explore further How analysis detects a hidden zero

A Mellin transform is a way to turn a function of a positive real number into a function of a complex exponent. The change u=log x makes it a Laplace transform. Differentiating many times weights the integral around a particular range of u, like a mathematical spotlight.

Focus the transform on a window

The kernel uᵏe⁻ᵘ is rescaled to have maximum 1 and its peak at relative coordinate u/k=1. It narrows in relative width as k grows.

Mathematical details

One sufficient ordinary-zeta criterion is: for each θ∈(½,1) there is a constant C such that for every Y₀ there exists Y≥Y₀ with G(y)≤Cyθ for every y∈[Y,Y3/2]. This implies RH. It is important that the estimate holds throughout each window, not just at a sequence of points.

For a hypothetical zero of real part β>½, choose ½<θ<min(β,1). The localized Landau argument uses high derivatives and residue information to exclude zeros beyond θ.
