PART III / THE PROOF

Why one half?

In Part II we found three defects in the lemniscate construction: its size is too large, rounding losses accumulate, and only selected primes are guaranteed to contribute. The proof replaces it with a family of maps that overcomes those defects.

Read forward from the construction to the final conclusion, or use the dependency diagram to work backwards. At every stage we will ask what it contributes to the same goal: bounding the centered prime sum G(x).

Two kinds of certainty. The formal proof is certified (21 November 2026). The classical guide by Rubinstein and collaborators, version 2, 22 November, does not yet derive the main arithmetic estimate from scratch. Our deeper explanations identify those gaps rather than silently assuming them. Sources checked 24 November 2026.

How to read the mathematics. Derived: argument supplied here. Established: a cited theorem with its scope. Formally verified: the checked formal result or a declaration's attributed audit. Illustrative: a finite example or model, not the theorem itself. Conditional reconstruction: a classical derivation with arithmetic inputs still quoted.

Two comparisons, two bridges

Why does the proof need so many different spaces? Its arithmetic evaluation and its independent geometric size bound live in different realizations. Two theta identities connect them.

This is a map of mathematical roles, not a diagram of isomorphisms. The period bridge identifies the map, the metric bridge controls its relative cost, and rank coverage is supplied separately. The classical explanation remains conditional on the upstream arithmetic and geometric inputs.

16 / SUPPLY THE DATA

Start with compatible data.

The construction has to work throughout whole windows, not at a few convenient numbers. First choose compatible fields, characters, and large families of residue classes.

Established

Auxiliary quadratic characters are selected using estimates in analytic number theory. Behrend-type sets of residue classes give the combinatorial supply. A cohomological window provides the uniform arithmetic controls; a host-existence theorem narrows that window to the one required by the analytic argument.

Choose many classes without a progression

The arithmetic supply uses sets that avoid short additive relations. Here encode triples of digits (0–4) as a number in base 10. Select a sphere in the digit cube to see why progressions disappear.

Exact finite Behrend example: 2b=a+c causes no carries in these digits, so an arithmetic progression of distinct numbers would give three collinear points on the same Euclidean sphere. Strict convexity forbids that. The proof needs larger sets avoiding more general bounded-coefficient relations and compatible arithmetic hosts.

Fit a complete window

Suppose the arithmetic data is good throughout [Y,Y²]. Can a shorter window [Z,Z3/2] fit inside it? Move Z=Yᵃ.

Exact interval containment for Y>1, shown on the logY scale. This illustrates why the argument needs uniform control throughout a window; the genuine host-supply theorem must also construct compatible data for every scale in it.

Explore further Why the quantifiers matter

A good construction at one point is not enough. For each small error tolerance and each sufficiently large starting scale, the arithmetic theorem supplies a later interval of compatible data. The analytic theorem then uses the whole interval. The two little experiments separate the combinatorial supply from this uniformity requirement.

Host supply is an actual formal implication (supplyChi); the window premise must itself be supplied. The diagrams below illustrate only the combinatorics and interval geometry.

Mathematical details — The quantifiers and the bridge

Fix a primitive character ψ. The property VWinAt ψ 401 supplies a cohomological window of the form [Y,Y²] under the stated scale conditions. The theorem supplyChi consumes that property to obtain HostExW(3/2) for the required data. These are quantified over sufficiently large scales; the choices may depend on x and on ψ, but the final family and window must satisfy the analytic criterion. A definition such as VWinAt is not by itself a theorem of existence.

17 / REPAIR THE SIZE

Build a family of maps.

The lemniscate numbers came with strong divisibility but poor size comparison. Replace the integer by a family of lattice maps, whose images have an arithmetic index and an analytic size.

Illustrative

U(X₀) → U(X₁) → ··· → U(Xₘ)

A theta kernel converts a function on one unitary group into one on another by integration or pairing. The arithmetic structure controls which denominators can appear; analytic structure controls its norm. The iterated lift has a final period which can be compared across different choices of intermediate twists.

The first bridge: the same evaluation

Think of a theta kernel as a table relating two spaces. Summing its entries in two compatible orders can compute the same pairing. In the actual proof, a seesaw identity lets finite arithmetic calculations of theta derivatives compute the automorphic period that later enters the rank comparison. It is not enough to construct another map with similar divisibility.

Explore further Why two towers?

At each step the point space grows by one more dimension than the row space. Their codimension gap decreases by one. Eventually the original period can be computed by a definite restriction at the top, using a chain of kernels whose derivatives have arithmetic meaning. The intervening periods alternate Bessel and Fourier–Jacobi types.

Mathematical details — The actual period and its quotient
dim Xⱼ = k+j(d+1),   dim Yⱼ = k+m−1+jd

The gap is m−1−j; at the endpoint the inclusion reverses and Yₘ has codimension one in Xₘ. At definite lattice points the CM Hom space has matrix tangent directions. In characteristic zero the Cauchy identity decomposes its symmetric powers into Schur modules of the two groups. This explains why theta derivatives have the tensor types of matrix kernels; it does not assert an integral splitting at every prime.

Π(Kₘ···K₁φ,F) = ζw,t Bw(φ)(ιtPqtF)

Here Kⱼ are the jet operators, Pq is the effective row quotient, ι its descent to the common row space, and ζ is one at the reference tuple and a local unit at the relevant switched place. The normalized identity makes the finite computation vanish on the kernel of descent and identifies it with the actual period Bw. A valuation proved against all the integral row generators therefore applies to the same period on the patched row lattice. The normalized seesaw identity, integral realization and image assertions are substantive inputs; rearrangement of finite sums alone does not prove them.

Reading: §III.4 and §III.10 of the classical reconstruction.

Compose a family of maps

A chain is more than a sequence of arrows: the final map depends on the intermediate choices. Here each stage is an integer matrix with determinant 2. Tap a stage to switch its map.

Exact two-dimensional toy maps A = [[2,1],[0,1]], B = [[1,0],[1,2]]. Each composite has index 16 in the same target lattice ℤ², although the image and size change. Actual theta lifts act on much richer spaces; this experiment illustrates composition and common target, not their construction.

Explore further From one integer to many maps

A matrix maps a grid into a subgrid. Its index counts how many residue classes of the target are missed; prime factors of that index can record divisibility. A composite map can preserve the common target while accumulating several stages of arithmetic information. Actual theta kernels supply much more structure than the integer-matrix example.

The integer-matrix chains below show the architecture of composition. The actual theta lifts and their integral and analytic properties are deeper formal inputs.

Mathematical details — The maps and their arithmetic data

In the classical reconstruction, each word w specifies successive choices of characters and maps. Its composite βw maps a glued source lattice Λwgl into a common target R∨. The architecture uses the same target for a family of maps, which is essential for the rank comparison below. The local theta packages, identities of integrals, local matching and compatible unitary groups are separate inputs, not consequences of the Gaussian toy sum.

18 / CREATE DIVISIBILITY

Count odd prime powers.

The lemniscate showed why inert primes give a special gain. The actual maps exploit a related local calculation, turning repeated derivatives into divisibility at odd prime powers.

Derived

a/(p−1)−a/(p²−1)=a(p⁻¹+p⁻³+p⁻⁵+···)

See the odd powers emerge

The factorial produces contributions at every power of p. Subtract the loss from the formal logarithm and compare what remains with the sum at odd powers alone.

Odd powersEven powers / loss

Exact Legendre sum and coefficient-loss bound ⌊(a−1)/(p²−1)⌋ in the formal-logarithm model below. The odd-power series is the leading term, not an exact equality of integer valuations. The genuine theta-kernel estimate requires additional arithmetic inputs.

Explore further Why the ramp is the right shape

For a derivative of order a, a factorial creates contributions from powers of p. The elliptic logarithm costs some of them. At an inert prime the surviving leading contribution is a times the sum of inverse odd prime powers. In the actual construction a weight w changes the scale to x=a/w, so the positive part a/pʰ−w equals w times the same ramp from Part II.

The formal-logarithm coefficient estimate below is an elementary derivation. The genuine kernel integrality and lattice comparison are separate established inputs.

Mathematical details — A local calculation with a formal logarithm

As a model take ℓ(X)=X+∑j≥1Xp2j/pj and its formal inverse U. Set L=p²−1. The coefficient of za in U has p-adic valuation at least −(a−1)/L. Induct on degree in U=z−∑Up2j/pj: a product of r=p2j coefficients costs at most (a−r)/L, and division by pj adds j≤(r−1)/L.

For integral f, the same product estimate applies to f(U(z)). The a-th derivative multiplies the coefficient by a!, whose valuation is ∑⌊a/ph⌋. Subtract the coefficient loss and obtain the displayed odd-power series up to O(1+logpa). The weighted lattice estimate introduces (a/ph−w)+=w(x/ph−1)+ when a=xw.

The model calculation is elementary; it does not establish integrality of the actual kernels or the local losses when passing to lattices. Part II §2 ↗, §III.5 ↗, §III.6 ↗.

19 / CONTROL THE SAME MAPS

Measure the size.

We now know what divides the maps. We must independently bound their size with enough accuracy that subtracting the prime main term leaves a small error.

Derived

∫₀∞ tae−tdt=a!

The Gaussian norm gives factorials. Stirling's expansion includes a log a − a, not merely a log a. Period formulas, inner-product formulas and lattice covolumes supply further constants that must match the arithmetic main term.

The second bridge: the cost of the evaluation

The finite row realization is good for evaluating derivatives, but is not generally a space of holomorphic sections whose degree we can estimate directly. The proof uses separate holomorphic realizations of matching arithmetic types. Their degrees provide genuinely new size information.

The Rallis inner-product formula measures a theta lift's squared norm using special L-values and local factors. In the required matching, each stage contributes a factor depending on the original representation. A corresponding auxiliary lift isolates the same factor. A scalar common to all types cancels from the relative size calculation.

Explore further Why a common scalar is harmless

Suppose every length in the row space is multiplied by 2. Its log covolume per dimension increases by log 2, while the norm of a fixed linear functional on it is divided by 2. Their logarithmic sum stays unchanged. If directions are rescaled differently, however, the volume depends on all of them while the norm may be governed by the most distorted direction.

log row volume per dimension + log dual-map norm

This is why the comparison needs the same relative norm factors on matched types, not merely equality of dimensions.

Mathematical details — Original-rank factors and determinant transport

At a good split place, a theta parameter update retains a twisted original multiset B and adds a prescribed string:

B′ = (zS/zT)B ⊔ {zSq(d−1)/2, …, zSq−(d−1)/2}

At s=(d+1)/2 the added string with the appropriate twist u contributes ∏a=1d(1−u q−a)−1. The original-rank spectral dependence is retained. Ratios of splitting characters are unaffected by a common twist. Switched and inert places need their own local integral and test-vector comparisons.

λdesc,π = κ ∏ⱼ λj,π

The squared norm factors refer to matched types π. This is not a tensor-product identification of lattices. In doubled slope conventions, scaling the squared row metric by c adds log c to the normalized row covolume and subtracts log c from the log squared norm of the dual evaluation. For a type-dependent change with relative eigenvalues λᵢ the increase is bounded by mean(log λᵢ)−log min λᵢ; it need not vanish.

At finite places an exact rational determinant comparison is needed. Divide the switched identity by the reference one in compatible algebraic bases before taking valuations. The shared analytic scalar cancels; one does not take a valuation of an L-value. The matching, common normalization, algebraic readings and finite-index bounds remain substantive inputs.

Reading: §III.4 and §III.7. The norm factorization is not a consequence of equivariance alone.

A second multiplicity–size argument inside the geometry

Where does the independent degree estimate come from? On a projective variety, hyperplanes cut out points. Sections that vanish to prescribed orders can be organized in layers. A determinant of sections must vanish whenever the final hyperplane passes through a point of the cut. The orders of vanishing force a factor of that determinant. Comparing its height with the sizes of short sections gives a degree bound.

One point, several layers

Illustration for a curve and section degree 4. Each bar unit is one power of the final hyperplane evaluated at a cut point. The actual auxiliary varieties and dimensions are different.

Explore further Why geometric multiplicity is independent of prime multiplicity

The outer divisor comes from prime valuations of a pairing. The inner divisor comes from geometric vanishing of polynomial section determinants. They are not the same divisor. The metric bridge makes the inner degree usable in the outer estimate. A large common determinant weight is restored using a CM period identity; the remaining geometry controls a smaller residual degree.

Mathematical details — The Chow determinant mechanism

For a projective component of dimension D and degree δ, a good cut by D hyperplanes meets δ distinct points. The Chow form f specializes, as a function of the last hyperplane ℓ, to a scalar times ∏Pℓ(P). In degree T, independent residue symbols indexed by α, |α|<T−D, acquire ℓ(P)T−|α| from the short section columns. Together with the deeper columns and complement, the filtration gives a nonzero polynomial determinant with factor fb.

b = ∑|α|<T−D(T−|α|)
(D+1)b = binom(T−1,D)[T+D(D+1)]

The counting identity follows by adjoining a slack coordinate and using symmetry of D+1 coordinates. In dimension one b=(T−1)(T+2)/2, giving 9 at T=4. One nonzero polynomial family is required: dense good cuts promote the specialized factors to a global divisor. Its actual polynomial height is additive under factorization. The product formula, short-column bounds and a separate cofactor-height allowance give the finite section-degree bound. This needs geometric regularity, short sections, projective-height and rank estimates; counting does not prove those inputs. The parallel determinant twist then restores the CM-period main term.

Reading: §III.7, geometric degree comparison. This explains the conditional mechanism, not a fresh proof of its uniform arithmetic geometry.

Measure the index with area

An integer map stretches the unit square into a parallelogram. The area counts the index of its image in the original integer lattice. Change the two diagonal entries and the shear.

Image latticeFundamental parallelogram

Exact matrix [[a,b],[0,d]]. Index = ad; the determinant is bounded by the product of column lengths and by the square of the operator norm. Shear increases the size bounds without increasing the index. The proof generalizes this comparison to slopes and covolumes.

Explore further Why the second estimate has to be sharp

A large index says that many primes divide the map; the determinant comparison says that the same index is at most the size permitted by its norm and lattice volumes. A rough upper bound leaves a gap as large as the count itself. The hard step is obtaining the predicted prime main term, including its linear correction, before the error is measured.

The determinant/covolume relation is exact; the sharp theta norm and covolume estimates are not derived by this small example.

Mathematical details — From one determinant to slopes

For an injective map A between equal-rank Euclidean lattices Λ and R, write I=[R:AΛ]. Then I·covol(R)=|det A|covol(Λ). If each singular value is at most L, log I≤d log L+log covol(Λ)−log covol(R). For unequal ranks the relevant target is the saturated image; the proof uses slope inequalities for lattices and controls the mean slope through a family. This identity is a finite-dimensional model of the comparison, not its replacement.

The actual size estimate uses theta inner products, period identities, local indices and dimension/covolume estimates. §III.4 ↗, §III.7 ↗ give the conditional derivation and enumerate the needed inputs.

20 / MAKE THE FAMILY LARGE

Don't count the same direction twice.

A single map might only account for a tiny part of the target. To get the sharp comparison we need a family whose images together occupy enough independent directions.

Derived

Even spanning the target is not quite enough. The strongest source directions might all land in the same small part of the target, while weak directions account for the rest. The required rank estimate survives discarding some source directions, and controls the distribution of their corrected slopes.

Explore further From rank to a weighted basis

Imagine sorting candidate image vectors by the strength of the bound each carries. Keep the strongest independent direction, then the next strongest that adds a dimension, and so on. The weight of the resulting basis measures more than whether a determinant is nonzero. The real argument uses the slope filtration of the actual integral lattices; it need not construct short integral basis vectors this way.

Mathematical details — Filtered determinant weight

Let A(t) be the span of the images of source slope directions with slope minus map height at least t. The integral-map slope inequality gives A(t) contained in the target slope step of threshold t. For target rank D, common threshold b and lower endpoint l=b−R below the minimum target slope, the completed filtration has determinant weight

W = Dl + ∫lb dim A(t) dt = Db − ∫lb codim A(t) dt.

The target degree is at least W. Integrating the robust rank bound therefore bounds b minus the mean target slope by a coverage error and the mean positive source deficits. The filtration weight is not itself an Arakelov degree of a new lattice. The actual robust rank estimate on the periods is an input; ordinary total rank alone does not suffice.

When do many maps give new information?

Imagine six unit vectors, each with a distinct direction plus a shared component. As the shared component grows, their images overlap more. The Gram matrix measures this directly.

Distinct directionsShared direction

Exact Gram matrix: diagonal 1, all other entries c. Its eigenvalues are 1+5c and five copies of 1−c. The effective-rank lower bound is 6/(1+5c²), not the actual rank unless at the endpoints. Relative trace estimates play the role of controlling overlaps in the real proof.

Explore further How independence improves the estimate

If every map has essentially the same image, adding more maps does not give the necessary coverage of the target. A Gram matrix distinguishes this case from genuinely independent images. Its second moment bounds how much repetition is possible. The real period estimates enforce the coverage needed by the lattice comparison.

The elementary Gram inequality is proved below. The actual relative trace estimates that control rank are additional inputs.

Mathematical details — A second-moment mechanism

For unit vectors v₁,…,vN spanning dimension r, let A=∑vᵢvᵢ*. Its eigenvalues are nonnegative, tr A=N and tr A²=∑|⟨vᵢ,vⱼ⟩|². Cauchy–Schwarz yields r≥N²/tr A². If every vector points in the same direction, the bound is 1; if they are orthogonal, it is N. Control of overlaps is therefore an effective way to prove a rank bound.

The actual proof uses relative trace-formula estimates for periods, together with the subspace and size conditions; this elementary Gram argument only illustrates the mechanism. §III.8 ↗; announcement item 16.

21 / CONTROL THE LOSSES

Keep the arithmetic integral.

The preceding comparison requires integral structures. Denominators can destroy a local gain, so the proof chooses several compatible paths and controls what is lost when combining them.

Illustrative

Combine two integral structures

One choice of intermediate map may have a bad denominator at 2, and another at 3. Consider two admissible sublattices d₁ℤ and d₂ℤ of the same target ℤ. Their sum keeps the smaller valuation at each prime.

First choiceSecond choiceSum of lattices

Exact one-dimensional model: d₁=2ᵃ3ᵇ, d₂=2ᵇ3ᵃ; their sum is gcd(d₁,d₂)ℤ. Bézout's identity produces the generator. The higher-dimensional theta problem has extra obstructions, controlled by cohomology; one cannot simply replace it with a gcd.

Explore further What could erase the gain?

If an intermediate transformation has a denominator divisible by p, powers of p gained from differentiation can be lost. Several paths to the common target allow different local choices. Their gluing must be compatible across primes, and the cohomological estimate bounds the remaining obstruction; the gcd example demonstrates only the local minimum principle.

The gcd calculation is exact in dimension one; cohomological loss bounds in the actual proof are deeper statements.

Mathematical details — The arithmetic obstruction

Changing an intermediate character may preserve the final period while changing its local denominators. One seeks a common integral structure built from these choices, so that no single choice of denominator controls the entire argument. The residual obstruction is measured using lengths of locally trivial Galois cohomology groups of twisted modules. Their weighted sum controls the denominator losses in the formal comparison.

The definition VVle requires the relevant local lengths to be finite and their logarithmically weighted sums bounded. The statement vvRecord converts this to a summable bound. The cohomology comparison itself is a deep input, not an elementary gcd identity. §III.9 ↗ and §III.11 ↗ distinguish the conditional calculation from the quoted theorem.

22 / ASSEMBLE THE COMPARISON

The main inequality.

Now the arithmetic and analytic sides meet. The lower bound from divisibility is a weighted prime sum P. The independent size comparison contains a predicted main term M plus errors. The question is whether P−M is small enough.

Conditional reconstruction

meanu ∑t,j(Pu,t,j−Mu,t,j) ≤ E
E/B ≤ xCε(x/m+xm/k+m+k/n²)

Here u translates a set of residue classes, t labels members of a family and j labels stages of a chain. The construction fixes weights w, positive derivative orders at and a normalizing quantity B. In the classical reconstruction the sums P contain nonnegative ramp contributions from prime powers. M is an independently derived main term.

The unknown target slope cancels

We do not need a sharp numerical estimate for the absolute size of the common target. The arithmetic enlargement makes each source's slope large; the auxiliary geometric comparison bounds the height of the same map relative to the target. Collective rank coverage prevents those corrected source slopes from being much larger than the target slope.

source slope − map height ≥ target slope + arithmetic gain − geometric budget − error
collective corrected slope ≤ target slope + coverage error

Combine these comparisons: the target slope disappears, leaving an upper bound on the arithmetic gain. The finite corrections and period normalizations in the two estimates must agree, not merely have the same approximate magnitude.

Explore further What has to cancel, and what must be bounded

There are distinct cancellations: the common norm multiplier cancels by duality; the signed finite correction connects the row and column realizations; the level and CM-period identities give the right main term; the common target slope cancels by collective coverage. Mathematical details — The common threshold

23 / RECOVER THE ORIGINAL COUNT

Recover all the primes.

The main inequality controls selected prime powers. Character comparison removes the restriction, and the normalized error budget shows why the square-root exponent is attainable.

Established

Pick auxiliary primes q and d. Let χq and χd be their quadratic characters, with η=χqχd. For r coprime to qd, the weight (1+χq(r))(1−χd(r)) selects numbers which are squares modulo q and nonsquares modulo d. It is 4 there and 0 elsewhere.

Rq,d(x)=G(x)+Gχq(x)−Gχd(x)−Gη(x)

This is an identity of centered sums, including the exceptional residues with their actual weights. Estimate B controls the three unwanted terms. Estimate C controls the cells counted at much larger scales. With (M), these give Estimate A for R.

24 / EXCLUDE AN OFF-LINE ZERO

A zero cannot hide.

The arithmetic work now gives an upper bound on the centered prime sum over arbitrarily late windows. We turn that upper bound into nonvanishing of the zeta function to the right of the critical line.

Established

G(x) ≤ Cxθ   on   [Y,Y3/2]

If this holds for arbitrarily large Y, for a fixed ½<θ<1, the window criterion rules out zeros with real part greater than θ. If we can do it for every such θ, RH follows.

A pole inside the forbidden circle

The one-sided estimate controls the high derivatives of a transform. A hypothetical pole too close to the evaluation point makes those derivatives grow faster than the allowed bound.

Allowed scaleSingle pole

Exact single-pole Taylor coefficient, with θ=0.60 and σ=3 fixed. Constants and other poles are omitted here; the localization and isolation argument below is what justifies this comparison for the real transform.

Explore further From growth to nonvanishing

The centered prime sum has a transform with a pole for every zeta zero. An off-line pole closer than the allowed radius would force sufficiently high Taylor coefficients to grow faster than the window bound permits. Localizing the transform makes the good intervals enough, despite the lack of bounds between them.

The one-sided window theorem is established. The pole calculation and proof outline below explain its mechanism.

Mathematical details — Derive the transform and the pole

For Re(s)>1 integrate each ramp separately:

∫n∞(x/n−1)x−s−1dx = n−s/[s(s−1)]
F(s)=−ζ′(s)/[s(s−1)ζ(s)]−1/(s−1)²+(1+γ)/(s−1)

The last two terms remove the singularity at 1. At a zero ρ of order ν, write ζ(s)=(s−ρ)νh(s), h(ρ)≠0. Then ζ′/ζ=ν/(s−ρ)+h′/h. The transform has residue −ν/[ρ(ρ−1)]. In the general positive family of characters the numerator is a positive weighted sum of multiplicities, so the pole cannot disappear.

Why windows are enough — classical proof outline

Set u=log x, g(u)=G(eu), and Aj(s)=(−1)jF(j)(s)/j!. For a large real σ, the integral weight is uje−σu/j!. At U=log Y choose j near 5(σ−2)U/4. The weight after using the global estimate |g(u)|≪e2u concentrates exponentially in [U,3U/2].

|Aj(σ+it)|+Aj(σ) ≤ 2∫ max(g(u),0)uje−σu/j! du

Only the positive part of g is needed. The window bound controls the integral inside; the exponentially small tails control the rest. Along these j the right-hand side is O((σ−θ)−j) for σ sufficiently large. If a pole lies beyond θ, choose a center z=σ+it with a unique nearest pole at distance d<min(σ−θ, distance from σ to its nearest pole). This is possible by moving t near the height of a suitable nonreal pole. Pick r strictly between d and that minimum. The pole contributes a term of order d−j; after subtracting it, Cauchy's estimate bounds the remainder and |Aj(σ)| by O(r−j). As d<r<σ−θ the two bounds contradict one another for large j.

This is the ordinary-zeta classical argument, with constants and tail estimates in Part II §1 ↗; the Lean route is not literally this derivation. See the localization experiment.

25 / REFLECTION

Exclude the other half.

The window theorem rules out every zero to the right of the critical line. The functional equation then rules out a zero to the left, because it would have a reflected partner on the right.

Formally verified

ζ(s) ≠ 0   when   Re(s) > ½, s ≠ 1

Remove one half of the plane

Place a hypothetical zero anywhere in the strip. Its reflected partners are forced by the symmetries.

Hypothetical zeroForced partners

This is an exact implication of the symmetries, not a search for new zeros. Turn on the theorem: any off-line candidate contradicts it, even if the candidate was initially on the left.

Explore further Why both halves are excluded

Complex conjugation reflects a zero across the horizontal axis. The functional equation reflects it across the vertical critical line after conjugation. Consequently an off-line zero on either side implies a zero strictly to the right. Excluding that half is sufficient; no numerical search is needed.

The final RH statement is checked in the original formal proof. The reflection implication is also derived below.

Mathematical details — The exact endpoint

The completed function ξ(s)=½s(s−1)π−s/2Γ(s/2)ζ(s) obeys ξ(s)=ξ(1−s). Its zeros are precisely the nontrivial zeros of ζ. Nonvanishing on the right therefore forces Re(s)=½. For Dirichlet functions the functional equation pairs a character with its conjugate. Imprimitive functions differ by finitely many factors 1−χ(p)p−s; these cannot vanish for Re(s)>0 because |χ(p)p−s|<1.

The five exported statements and their exact scope are recorded by Sutherland. They do not establish GRH for all automorphic L-functions.

26 / CLOSE THE LOOP

Why the proof works.

Start with the three defects exposed by the lemniscate. The theta maps repair the size mismatch; the family and chain share arithmetic losses; character comparison removes the restriction to inert primes. The main inequality joins these repairs into one estimate. Complete windows let the analytic theorem exclude every zero to the right, and reflection rules out the left.

The most striking aspect is the change of object: a question about zeros of a complex function is answered by finding enough arithmetic maps with the right divisibility and size.
